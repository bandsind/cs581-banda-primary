# Attack Surface Map — IT Layer (Level 4 / Corporate)
## CS 581 Workshop 1 | Sindi Banda | September 5, 2026

**System:** OT Security Information & Event Management (OT-SIEM)

**System's Purdue zone:** Level 3, Site Operations

**Purdue zone mapped in this layer:** Level 4, Corporate / Enterprise Network

**Regulatory classification:** Critical Digital Asset (CDA) under 10 CFR 73.54. OT-SIEM performs a security monitoring function that protects SSEP-supporting systems from cyber attack, which meets CDA identification criterion 5 of the six listed in RG 5.71 Rev. 1 Section C.3.1.3, covering digital assets that protect any of the listed asset types from a cyberattack up to and including the DBT.

**Regulatory basis for the function itself:** RG 5.71 Rev. 1 Section C.4.1 is the only place in the guide that names SIEM directly. It lists continuous monitoring of inbound and outbound network traffic and analysis of event logs, and automated collection and analysis of network traffic and CDA events and logs to provide real-time notification of security events and support incident response and forensic analysis, then states that these actions support anomaly detection by a HIDS, NIDS, or SIEM.

**Note on RG 5.71 level numbering:** RG 5.71 uses a five-level defensive architecture in which Level 4 is the innermost and most secure and the corporate network sits at a lower level. This is inverted relative to Purdue numbering used elsewhere in this map. Where a claim below is sourced to RG 5.71, it is stated in RG 5.71 terms.

**Note on architecture scope:** Rows marked with an asterisk are generic OT-SIEM capabilities described in NIST SP 800-82r3 Appendix E. The CS 581 Plant Architecture Diagram does not show them for this plant. They are included as potential interfaces, not confirmed ones.

## IT Layer Attack Surface

| Component | Role | Threat Vectors | Notable Gaps | Confidence & Provenance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OT-SIEM to corporate SOC and ICT-SIEM log/alert forwarder | Exports correlated OT security events and alerts from OT-SIEM to the corporate SOC and ICT-SIEM for enterprise-wide visibility and reporting. This is the one outbound edge the CS 581 Plant Architecture Diagram shows, labeled as a one-way export across the separation boundary | The forwarder is the boundary itself. OT-SIEM sits at a higher RG 5.71 defensive level than the corporate network, so an export outward is the permitted direction. The risk is the return leg. If the forwarder is implemented as a two-way management channel rather than a one-way export, response or control data travels inward from a lower level to a higher one, which is what the boundary device exists to block | Cannot confirm whether the forwarder is truly unidirectional in a given facility's implementation or supports a query-back channel for remote diagnostics | Documented: NIST SP 800-82r3 Appendix E.2.1 (Centralized Logging); NRC RG 5.71 Rev. 1 Section C.3.2.1 (one-way data flow allowed from Level 4 to Level 3 and Level 3 to Level 2); CS 581 Plant Architecture Diagram (OT-SIEM to ICT-SIEM, one-way export) |
| Data diode / unidirectional gateway at the boundary | Enforces one-way data flow at the boundary so no session can be initiated inward from the corporate network | A data diode alone does not satisfy defense-in-depth. RG 5.71 states that exploits associated with supply chain, portable media and mobile devices, wireless, and physical presence pathways can circumvent the protection a data diode provides, and that a diode gives no protection where policy is violated or protection mechanisms are bypassed | Cannot confirm whether the diode is a single device or a redundant pair, its fail-safe behavior, or its exact physical location relative to the CDA boundary | Documented: NRC RG 5.71 Rev. 1 Section C.3.2 (Defense-in-Depth Protective Strategies, data diode limitations); NRC RG 5.71 Rev. 1 Section C.3.2.1 (hardware-enforced unidirectional links preferred where one-way communication is required) |
| Vendor remote support and diagnostic access to the OT-SIEM management interface * | Lets the OT-SIEM vendor tune correlation rules, apply patches, and troubleshoot the platform without a technician on site | Vendor access for rule tuning and troubleshooting is a standing remote pathway from the corporate network. Without MFA and session recording, it is a direct route to the SIEM's own configuration. RG 5.71 requires that initiation of communications from a lower security level to a CDA at a higher level be handled on a deny-all, permit-by-exception basis with documented justification and a security risk analysis, so this pathway must be an analyzed exception rather than a default | Cannot confirm whether MFA, session recording, and time-limited access are actually enforced on this path, or whether a shared or standing credential is used | Documented: NIST SP 800-82r3 Section 6.2.10 (Remote Access); NRC RG 5.71 Rev. 1 Section C.3.2.1 (deny-all, permit-by-exception); NRC RG 5.71 Rev. 1 Section C.3.1.4 (review and validation of connectivity pathways) |
| Vendor software update/patch channel for the SIEM platform * | Delivers signed software and detection-rule updates to keep the OT-SIEM platform's logic current | The update mechanism of a monitoring platform is itself a supply chain target. SolarWinds Orion, a network monitoring platform with a comparable centralized visibility role, was compromised at the build stage and used to gain footholds across federal and critical infrastructure networks through a routine update. RG 5.71 addresses this case directly, requiring that updates to an asset behind a data diode be implemented so the update does not contain known malware and its integrity is maintained during transport | Cannot confirm the specific SIEM product deployed at a given facility, its current patch level, or its update-signing verification process | Documented: CISA Activity Alert AA20-352A / Emergency Directive 21-01 (SolarWinds Orion supply chain compromise); NIST SP 800-82r3 Section 6.2.11 (Flaw Remediation and Patch Management); NRC RG 5.71 Rev. 1 Section C.3.2.1 (update integrity behind a data diode) |
| Shared time synchronization (NTP) source * | Synchronizes OT-SIEM's internal clock with a reference time source so correlated events across sources carry consistent, orderable timestamps | If the OT-SIEM's time source traces through a corporate-facing NTP server rather than a source inside its own security level, that low-bandwidth service is a persistent path across the boundary | Cannot confirm the actual NTP source hierarchy in use at any specific facility | Inferred: NIST SP 800-82r3 Section 6.2.12 (Time Synchronization) names the control area; no source confirms a specific facility's architecture |
| Shared identity and authentication (AD/LDAP/SSO) for SOC analyst logins * | Authenticates SOC analysts and administrators who log into the OT-SIEM console | Where the same analysts monitor both IT and OT alerts, OT-SIEM logins sometimes ride the corporate identity provider instead of a separate OT credential store. A compromised corporate account would then reach the OT-SIEM console directly | Cannot confirm whether OT-SIEM login credentials are separated from corporate Active Directory at a specific site | Inferred: NIST SP 800-82r3, Executive Summary, defense-in-depth bullet list (p. 4), names separate authentication mechanisms and credentials for OT versus corporate network users as recommended practice, and states that OT network accounts do not use corporate network user accounts. This is a documented recommendation, not a confirmed configuration |
| Web-based SIEM analyst console/dashboard * | Presents correlated alerts, dashboards, and investigation tools to analysts through a browser | If the console is hosted in or routed through a boundary DMZ rather than confined to OT-SIEM's own security level, it becomes a browser-based attack surface (credential theft, session hijacking, unpatched web framework flaws) reachable from the wider corporate network | Cannot confirm the console's actual network placement, or whether it is reachable from general corporate workstations | Inferred: NIST SP 800-82r3 Appendix E.2 (Network Monitoring, SIEM) documents the console's existence, not its placement |
| Vendor cloud analytics/threat intelligence telemetry egress * | Sends anonymized telemetry to the vendor's cloud service for cross-customer threat correlation and enrichment, where the feature is enabled | An outbound telemetry channel is the permitted direction under RG 5.71, but any enrichment returned to OT-SIEM is inbound from a lower level and would need to be an analyzed deny-all, permit-by-exception case. A cloud service also places the far end outside the licensee's control entirely, which no part of the RG 5.71 defensive architecture contemplates | Cannot confirm whether any licensed facility has this feature enabled at all; it may not exist in this environment | Theoretical: extrapolated from general commercial OT security platform architecture (industry vendor comparisons, 2026); no NRC or NIST source reviewed addresses cloud egress from a nuclear OT monitoring platform |

## Summary

The dominant IT-layer risk for OT-SIEM is that every path required for its stated function, log export to the corporate SOC, vendor patching, and threat intel enrichment, is also a path that can carry data inward from a lower security level to a higher one if it is implemented as an ordinary two-way integration instead of a purpose-built one-way one. RG 5.71 Rev. 1 does not flatly forbid those inbound paths, it requires them to be handled deny-all, permit-by-exception with documented justification and a security risk analysis, which means the compliance question is whether each one was analyzed rather than whether it exists. The primary source of uncertainty is that public regulatory and technical guidance describes what the architecture must do, not how any specific licensee's OT-SIEM deployment is actually wired, so most rows above rest on inference from the rule rather than confirmation of an implementation.
